 4                                                        Abstract
 5           We introduce annihilator sketching, a technique for handling vertex deletions directly on graph
 6       sketches. After the sketches have been formed, a referee can algebraically eliminate all edges incident
 7       to a specified set of vertices, without recovering those edges or accessing the original graph. Supporting
 8       𝑘 vertex deletions incurs only an additive 𝑂(𝑘) overhead in sketch dimension.
 9           We apply this technique to vertex connectivity in the distributed graph sketching model, where each
10       vertex of an 𝑛-vertex graph simultaneously sends a single message, computed from its neighborhood,
11       to a referee. Annihilator sketching yields√protocols for 𝑘-vertex connectivity using 𝑂(𝑘 log3 𝑛) bits
12       per vertex with public randomness and 𝑂( 𝑘𝑛 log3/2 𝑛) bits per vertex with private randomness. Both
13       protocols succeed with high probability and construct sparse subgraphs preserving connectivity after
14       every set of fewer than 𝑘 vertex failures. The public-randomness bound improves the 𝑂(𝑘 log4 𝑛) bound
15       obtained from Assadi and Shah (SOSA 2023), resolving a question of Assadi. The private-randomness
16       protocol extends the approach of Holm et al. (FOCS 2019) from ordinary connectivity to vertex connec-
17       tivity. Our public-randomness construction also yields a one-pass dynamic streaming algorithm using
18       𝑂(𝑘𝑛 log3 𝑛) bits of space.
                                                                                             √
19           We complement these randomized protocols with a lower bound of Ω(𝑘 + 𝑛) on the maximum
20       message length of any deterministic protocol for 𝑘-vertex connectivity, for 1 ≤ 𝑘 ≤ 𝑛/6. This bound
21       holds even for deciding whether the graph is 𝑘-vertex-connected, without requiring a certificate. In par-
                                                                                                              √
22       ticular, for ordinary connectivity it improves the Ω(𝑛1/3 ) bound of Robinson and Tan (2026) to Ω( 𝑛).

69    4        A New Sketching Primitive
70    Previous protocols for 𝑘-vertex connectivity in [GMT15; AS23], as well as for related problems such as the
71    even more fundamental connectivity problem, [AGM12; HKTZZ19] all rely on maintaining linear sketches
72    of each node’s row in the signed incidence matrix of 𝐺. The streaming and public randomness dynamic
73    sketching protocols maintain an 𝓁0 sampler sketch of this vector, while the private randomness distributed
74    sketching and deterministic protocols maintain a sparse recovery sketch.
75         All of these protocols proceed by building up “blobs” of nodes, which eventually form the components
76    of a graph. When using rows of the signed incidence matrix, the useful property is that for an edge {𝑢, 𝑣} ∈ 𝐸
77    with 𝑢 < 𝑣, coordinate {𝑢, 𝑣} of 𝑢’s row will have a +1 while the same coordinate in 𝑣’s row will have a
78    −1. Therefore, taking linear sketches of these rows, and summing up the sketches from all nodes within
79    a certain blob cancel out all of the interior edges, leaving only the boundary edges. Then, 𝓁0 sampling or
80    sparse recovery can be used to recover edges from this boundary, allowing the blob to be built up further.
81         Our main contribution in this work is to introduce a new primitive based on sketching the signed
82    adjacency matrix instead of the signed incidence matrix. Initially, this might seem counterintuitive based
83    on how the protocols operate, since these sketches cannot be summed up to remove interior edges; an
84    edge {𝑖, 𝑗} will now appear in the 𝑗 th coordinate of 𝑖’s row and the 𝑖th coordinate of 𝑗’s row. However,
85    we will show that these sketches can be postprocessed—without knowledge of the graph—such that they
86    act as a sketch of a scaling of the signed incidence matrix. Thus, summing up the sketches of a set of
87    nodes eliminate the set’s interior edges. This allows our new primitive to recover the results of [AGM12;
88    HKTZZ19]. Furthermore, the main benefit of this new primitive is that for any set of nodes 𝐹 ⊆ 𝑉 with
89    |𝐹 | ≤ 𝑘, revealed after sketching, the sketches can be processed—again without knowledge of the graph—
90    such that they sketch a scaling of the signed incidence matrix of 𝐺[𝑉 ⧵ 𝐹 ], with only an additive 𝑘 factor to
91    the sketch dimension. This allows us—as shown in Section 5.1—to solve the 𝑘-vertex-connectivity problem
92    without any graph subsampling, improving on [AS23] in the dynamic streaming and public randomness
93    distributed sketching settings, and give the first protocol in the private randomness distributed sketching
 94   setting.

97    uses, such as constructing 𝓁0 samplers. The rest of the section explains how the sketches are computed.

316   5.3   Turnstile Streams
317   Our public randomness algorithm above can also be implemented in the turnstile streaming model. Here,
318   instead of each node computing a sketch from their neighborhood, the algorithm maintains each node’s
319   sketch dynamically and in parallel. As our sketches are linear, this can be done simply and efficiently on
320   edge updates. At the end of the stream, the algorithm post-processes all sketches in the same way as the
